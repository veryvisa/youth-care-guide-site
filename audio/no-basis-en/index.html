This is the brief on matters with no legal basis and common misunderstandings. We clarify rights in BC legislation on health care, schools, and child protection, resolving misconceptions where no legal basis was found. First, minors and consent. Under Section 17 of the Infants Act, there's no minimum consent age.

The test is maturity, and emergency treatment happens at any age. Second, involuntary admissions. A diagnosis isn't enough. All four criteria in Section 22.3 of the Mental Health Act must be met. Police taking someone to a hospital under Section 28.1 and 2 is for examination only.

Without a certificate, they must be released. Third, schools must continue educational programs, even if a student's excluded under Section 91 of the School Act. In child protection, confidentiality has statutory reporting exceptions. Self-destructive behavior signals emotional harm under Section 13.2 of the Child, Family, and Community Service Act.

Hospital interpretation, duties, and learning plan substituting IEPs aren't verified. Under Sections 22 and 23 of the Mental Health Act, a valid first certificate authorizes apprehension and 48-hour detention. A second certificate by a different physician extends it to one month, renewable under Section 24. Declarations in 2026 BCSC 1416 concern treatment without consent suspended for six months.

Who decides? Youth 16 or over decide voluntary admission. Physicians decide certificates and maturity. Directors must refuse disclosing a reporter's identity without consent. Voluntary patients 16 or over authorize their own treatment under Section 20.8.

For involuntary patients, under the current text, the director may sign consent to treatment forms. The ministry's position presupposes that the patient is not capable. And the court has declared the scheme unconstitutional, though suspended.
