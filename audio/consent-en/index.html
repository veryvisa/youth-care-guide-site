This is the brief on consent to treatment and the changing treatment authority. Today, we're outlining British Columbia law on medical consent for capable minors, court overrides for refused necessary treatment, and recent changes to involuntary psychiatric care authorizations. First, under Section 17 of the Infants Act, a minor can consent to their own health care with no minimum age.

The health care provider decides if the minor is capable. They must explain and be satisfied the minor understands the nature, consequences, reasonably foreseeable benefits, and risks, and conclude care is in the infant's best interests. If validly given, a parent's objection doesn't by itself defeat the minor's consent. Parents must go through a court process.

Second, shifting from capable consent to when necessary treatment is refused. Under Section 29 of the Child, Family, and Community Service Act, If care is refused that two medical practitioners believe necessary to preserve life or prevent serious impairment, a director may apply to court. Notice is given at least two days before to each parent and the capable child.

Also, involuntary patients can request a second medical opinion, completed as soon as reasonably practicable, and delivered to the director no later than two clear days after examination. Finally, moving to the specific shifting landscape of involuntary psychiatric care. A July 2026 judgment, 2026 BCSC 1416 declared the Mental Health Act doesn't empower directors

to authorize forced psychiatric treatment. The unconstitutional declarations are suspended six months, ending around late January 2027. How this applies to minors is plainly not verified. In this wider context, the Representative for Children and Youth recommended the upcoming review of the Mental Health Act consider a separate Mental Health Act or a separate part for children and youth.
